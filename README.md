# The River and the Canyon

A pair of essays that build a physical analogy for how large language models work — and then stress-test it.

## The papers

**1. The River and the Canyon**
A physical analogy for a transformer: weights as a frozen mountain, activations as water moving over fixed stone, training as the slow carving of the rock. The second half deliberately tries to break the analogy to find where it stops being the territory.
→ [`the-river-and-the-canyon/`](the-river-and-the-canyon/)

**2. What Kind of Water Carves the Mountain?** *(companion)*
The first paper followed only one river: human language. This one asks what changes when the water changes — code, video, simulation, action — and finds a second, distinct axis underneath the obvious one. Not just *which* data carved a capability (provenance), but how much precision that capability needs to survive (fragility), with quantization as the instrument that exposes it. The compact claim: **provenance shapes the mix, not the fate.**
→ [`what-kind-of-water/`](what-kind-of-water/)

Each folder contains the paper in Markdown, PDF, and Word formats. Read them in order; the second assumes the first.

Paper 1's folder also includes **No Mountain in the Sentence** — a short companion essay on the *method* behind both papers: the discipline for trusting an analogy exactly as far as it earns. It states one rule (say the claim with no mountain in the sentence) and three questions, then earns them by applying them to the mountain analogy itself. The papers are the worked example; this is the tool.

## On method

Both papers follow the same discipline: the analogy is allowed to be vivid only as long as the mechanism underneath it stays in view. Claims are sorted by how well the evidence actually supports them, the limits are stated plainly, and where the work reaches past what's established it says so. The picture is a way of asking sharper questions, not a machine for predicting what will work.
