# What the Boundary Venn Reveals

*A governance note, not a research finding. This records what the Method · Evaluation · Mechanism Venn contributed to the project's structure — not a new claim about models. It is the spatial form of the rule the Implications Index states in prose: the durable contribution is method and measurement, not new mechanism.*

**Status: v1.0 — locked, pre-Tier 0. A framework-level insight, recorded to prevent backsliding.**

---

## The keeper

> The Venn does not restrict the project; it reveals it. By blocking mechanism, it converts one unanswerable "why" into a family of answerable "how much, how often, under what conditions" questions.

**Rule:** If it requires circuits, it is not this project.

That is the finding. Everything below supports it.

---

## What the diagram implies

**1. The project occupies Method ∩ Evaluation.**
It measures behavioral stress-retention in service of ML evaluation. That overlap — the core space — is where Tier 0 lives, and its output is measurement, not theory: ΔR, same-error rate, rescue delta, and related behavioral quantities.

**2. Mechanism is intentionally inactive.**
The method cannot identify circuits, literal defects, grooves, or internal causes. The mechanism circle is dashed because it is *not this project's coordinate system* — not because it is future work the project intends to do. The Method ∩ Mechanism overlap is blocked: behavior cannot become a circuit claim. The river/canyon analogy lived there. The pressure tests killed it. The overlap stays blocked.

**3. The empty center is a design feature, not a gap.**
There is no claim that combines measurement, evaluation, and mechanism at once. A standard Venn treats the center as the most interesting place. This one treats it as the most dangerous — it is hatched and empty on purpose, because a project that claims to measure behavior, answer robustness, and name mechanism simultaneously is overclaiming by construction.

**4. The boundary reveals the real research program.**
Blocking mechanism does not leave the project empty. It exposes a dense set of valid, answerable, behavioral questions:

* **Tier 0 — now:** Does ΔR exist under quantization? Does same-error survival appear?
* **Tier 0.5 — if signal:** Does ΔR generalize across a second stress?
* **Tier 1 — later:** Does retention predict downstream reliability better than peak accuracy? (the A8 question) Do rescue effects reveal useful behavioral classes?

None of those requires claiming circuits. The boundary is a focusing device, not a limitation.

## Inference vs. exploration

The Venn separates two modes that are often confused.

**Inference, blocked:** "The model fails under stress; therefore it has fragile circuits." This moves from Method ∩ Evaluation into Mechanism. It crosses the dashed line, and the diagram forbids it.

**Exploration, enabled:** "The model fails under stress; measure how much, how often, under which stresses, and with which behavioral repairs." This stays inside the core space. It does not explain the failure; it maps its behavioral contours.

The boundary makes exploration *safe*. Without it, every measurement tempts a mechanistic inference: this looks like a defect, so perhaps the model contains one. With the boundary in place, the project can explore stress-retention freely because it has already committed not to infer circuits, defects, grooves, or internal causes from behavioral evidence alone.

This is why the project can ask many small questions instead of one oversized one. The big question — "why does this fail internally?" — requires crossing into mechanism. The smaller questions — "how much does it fail, how often, under what stress, and does an intervention change it?" — remain inside the measurement boundary.

The diagram does not limit curiosity; it channels it. It says: explore the core space exhaustively, but do not infer beyond it. That is what turns the boundary from a restriction into a research program — boundary-first does not mean curiosity-last; it means curiosity is pointed at the questions the method can actually answer.

## Why this is worth preserving

The Venn's contribution was not a model claim — it was a framework-level insight, and one of the most transferable things this project produced: **behavioral measurement is complete work, not incomplete mechanism.** If a method cannot see circuits, its conclusions cannot mention them. The geometry generalizes to any measurement project: a robustness benchmark, a safety eval, or a capability probe. Use one solid circle for the method, one for the field it serves, one dashed circle for the field it does *not* do, with the mechanism overlaps blocked.

It also guards against a specific, predictable backslide. Without this note, a future reader — possibly the author — may look at the Venn and conclude, "this means we should build the MI handoff now." It does not. Mechanism is handoff territory only, and only if Tier 0 produces signal; what travels is measurement output — ΔR, same-error, rescue — not a theory of internal cause. The project lives in Method ∩ Evaluation. That is the boundary, and it stays intact.

## What this is not

* It is not a claim that mechanism is unimportant.
* It is not a permanent refusal to collaborate with mechanistic interpretability.
* It is not a limitation waiting to be overcome by better wording.
* It is not evidence for circuits, defects, grooves, or internal structure.

## What this is

* A scope definition for this method.
* A protection against overclaiming.
* A way to make behavioral measurement complete on its own terms.
* A framework other measurement projects can borrow.

## The governance principle the project earned

> Draw the boundary first. Measure inside it. Do not claim outside it.
